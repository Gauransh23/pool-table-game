# Sensory feedback and control: primary-source scout

Scope: distinct experimental evidence for pool cueing, overlays, spin, sound, and visual perception. This is a source scout, not evidence that the current game exhibits a particular defect. The recommendations below are hypotheses to test in this game, not conclusions established by these studies.

## Findings

- **Cue delivery is a time-varying movement, but the right desktop mapping is untested.** A study recorded 3D cue acceleration and velocity in six expert, seven intermediate, and 14 novice cue-sport players on cue-ball and object-ball placement tasks. Adding an object ball increased cue-kinematic variability in every group, least for experts. [Source: motor control study, abstract, p. 1](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/resource_files/aiming_research_study.pdf). A separate study measured 20 elite players performing 18 follow, draw, stop and break tasks with eight 250 Hz cameras and a 5,000 Hz camera. [Source: Movement Variability of Professional Pool Billiards Players, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705815014976). These are physical cue studies, not controlled comparisons of mouse, drag, gamepad, or touch schemes. They do not justify adding simulated random error.
- **Pool overlays have a concrete precedent but a narrow accuracy claim.** A projector/Kinect system detects the table, balls, cue direction and strike and displays predicted trajectories and menus on the table. Its authors report 98% shot prediction accuracy *when no bouncing occurs*. [Source: Sousa et al. 2016, abstract](https://www.sciopen.com/article/10.1007/s41095-016-0047-3). The abstract does not establish that overlays improve game enjoyment, learning transfer, bank-shot accuracy, or that its predictor represents arbitrary spin. An earlier wearable system proposed ranked shots and overlaid ball paths; its first figure is explicitly the **desired** output, not proof of user performance improvement. [Source: Jebara et al. 1997, pp. 1–2](https://sites.cc.gatech.edu/home/thad/p/032_20_ARVR/stochastic_ISWC97.pdf#page=1).
- **Sound influences perceived contact.** In an experiment with 28 volunteers and 480 trials each, subjects judged whether moving discs streamed past or bounced. A sound at coincidence increased bounce reports (main effect p < .001), with trajectory history and offset also affecting reports. [Source: Grove et al. 2016, methods and results, pp. 457–461](https://www.yorku.ca/harris/pubs/grove.pdf#page=5). This was an ambiguous-disc psychophysics task, not pool-game quality testing; it does not establish an optimal audio delay or prove the sound changed sensory sensitivity rather than response criterion. It supports testing event-aligned contact audio against mismatched or absent audio.
- **Impact acoustics carry more information than a single fixed loudness.** RealImpact captures 150,000 recordings of 50 real objects with annotated impact positions, microphone positions, material and force profiles; its controlled hammer experiment shows a changed striker affects the response. [Source: Clarke et al. 2023, abstract, §§4 and appendix C](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09944). This is an everyday-object dataset, not a calibrated pool-ball/rail/cloth set; it supplies neither a billiards impulse-to-volume formula nor proof that synthesis beats samples. A distinct pitch experiment had 12 people in its first condition time a baffle against a falling ball; reported speed perception varied with pitch. [Source: Wang et al., abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34520509/). It does not authorize arbitrary pitch mapping for pool impacts.
- **Spin needs an observable surface feature or an explicit representation.** In table tennis, researchers isolated a ball logo in event-camera data and estimated optical flow; reported mean magnitude error was 10.7 ± 17.3 revolutions/s and mean axis error 32.9 ± 38.2°. [Source: Gossard et al. 2024, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09870). This is a flying table-tennis ball, not a pool rendering study, and does not show which markings gamers prefer. It illustrates why a visually uniform sphere cannot communicate rotation by its silhouette alone; test a readable surface mark against unobtrusive spin indicators.
- **Expertise and perspective require care.** Researchers compared seven expert, seven intermediate and 15 novice snooker players on general visual and sport-specific tests; the abstract reports no significant expert–novice differences on standard acuity, eye balance, color or depth tests, and interprets expertise as task-specific processing. [Source: Abernethy et al. 1994, abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/acp.2350080302). A billiards eye-tracking study used an occluded dynamic trajectory prediction task and reported expertise-related gaze differences. [Source: Crespi et al. 2012, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23115218/). Neither compares game cameras or establishes an optimal field of view; no direct evidence found here that a particular perspective or shadow improves pool-game aiming.

## Testable design implications and gaps

1. Prototype aim, power and contact point as separately inspectable inputs; compare a pull-and-release gesture with existing controls for repeatability, overshoot, shot time and player preference. Physical cue data do not select a digital mapping.
2. Offer an optional aim/first-contact overlay, derived from the actual shot model and clearly limited to what it predicts. Compare with no overlay across straight, cut and bank shots; measure both performance and preference.
3. Trigger distinct cue-ball, ball-ball and cushion audio at their actual events and compare velocity/impulse-conditioned sample variants with fixed samples in a blinded listening test. Obtain suitable pool-specific recordings before claiming authenticity. Avoid inferring exact gain, pitch or perceptual latency from the cited studies.
4. Compare actual rotation marks, a temporary spin diagram and no indicator for interpreting follow, draw and sidespin, including small-screen readability. Test camera angles with the same shot layouts, including occlusion and depth judgments.

Evidence gaps: no primary study located here compares desktop pool input schemes, calibrates ball/cloth/rail recordings to collision impulse, validates a spin overlay for billiards, or demonstrates a preferred pool-game camera. The linked abstracts for the elite movement and snooker perception studies were available, but their full methods were not checked; do not treat abstracts as complete study audits.
