# Cue-Sports Instruction and Measurement Source Scout

## Scope and verdict

This scout adds first-party instructor standards, coaching materials, vocabulary sources, demonstration footage, and practical measurement references. It does not revisit the prior Dr. Dave main-site / TP / common-speed-page coverage, WPA equipment specifications, or the Shepard monograph.

The useful new sources are the [Professional Billiard Instructor Association (PBIA) certification program and manual](https://playbetterbilliards.com/becomepbiainstructor/), the [WPBSA coaching qualifications](https://www.wpbsa.com/participation/coaching/qualifications-and-levels/) and [WPBSA “The Basics” guide](https://wpbsa.com/wp-content/uploads/WPBSA-The-Basics-updated-AMEND-MAY-2021-002.pdf), and the [APA league terminology glossary](https://ss.apaleagues.com/CustomPage.aspx?q=8222). They are good evidence for what instructors teach and how one league uses terms. They do not establish a universal numerical scale for stroke length, softness, touch, or power.

For measurement, the PBIA requires video capability at accredited academies and recommends speed sensors / other instrumentation, but publishes no sensor protocol. Original biomechanics studies show what controlled measurement can look like. A practical pool-table stimpmeter method exists, but its author explicitly says the method is not in widespread use. Treat measured outcomes, coach recommendations, league glossary wording, and equipment standards as separate evidence types.

## Vocabulary: what is and is not standardized

| Term | Source-grounded usage | Status / caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Draw / backspin** | APA’s glossary describes draw as backward cue-ball rotation imparted below the equator; a draw shot has enough remaining backspin at object-ball contact to reverse the cue ball. | League glossary description. It distinguishes the spin state (“draw”) from the shot outcome (“draw shot”). |
| **Follow / topspin** | APA describes follow as forward rotation produced by striking above the cue-ball equator; its “follow shot” wording describes forward travel after object-ball contact. WPBSA uses “topspin.” | Coaching/game vocabulary, not a governing rule definition. |
| **Stun** | WPBSA’s beginner guide presents stun among the main cue-ball spin controls, describes cue contact from just above backspin to around center, and says the cue ball may stop or nearly stop before following through. PBIA curriculum groups “tangent/stun line.” | Coach shorthand depends on the intended state at object-ball contact and shot geometry. The WPBSA description is pedagogical, not a precision physical definition. A study below uses a specific operational “stun” task definition; do not assume it is a universal glossary definition. |
| **Pocket speed** | APA’s glossary explicitly gives two senses: (1) the object-ball speed just sufficient to reach and drop into the target pocket; and (2) a pocket’s greater acceptance of a slightly imperfectly aimed ball at a soft speed. | Ambiguous. For a simulator parameter, use the first sense and record it as the minimum successful object-ball entry speed for a specified shot/table/pocket setup. The second is a pocket-behavior description. |
| **Short / medium / long** | PBIA teaches varying stroke length to obtain desired speed. In the one-player snooker biomechanics protocol below, “short” and “long” label two fixed object-ball-distance setups. | No shared cutoffs or universal stroke-length categories surfaced in the reviewed PBIA/WPBSA curriculum, rules, or APA glossary. The study’s labels are experiment-specific, not standards. Store measured cue travel and ball distance instead of assigning global bins unless a project defines its own thresholds. |
| **Touch / soft / firm / power** | APA defines speed control as using the correct cue-ball speed for position play and defines “timing” in terms of generating cue power through well-timed cue acceleration. PBIA lists “control and power” as a teaching topic. | These sources do not give universal thresholds in cue-tip speed, cue-ball speed, force, stroke length, or distance. They are qualitative coaching terms unless a task defines an operational scale. The words “soft/medium/hard” in a slow-motion tip video below refer to tip hardness, not shot power. |
| **Table speed** | APA’s glossary calls it a subjective assessment of how quickly balls roll over the cloth. Joe Waldron’s pool-table test separates a no-rail cloth roll from a rail-return test that reflects cloth plus cushion response. | A subjective term in common league usage; no global rating standard was found in the instructional/measurement sources reviewed. Record the exact test and conditions rather than only “fast” or “slow.” |

Sources for the glossary entries: [APA Basic Pool Terminology](https://ss.apaleagues.com/CustomPage.aspx?q=8222), [PBIA Instructor Manual](https://playbetterbilliards.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/PBIA_Instructor_Manual.pdf), [WPBSA “The Basics”](https://wpbsa.com/wp-content/uploads/WPBSA-The-Basics-updated-AMEND-MAY-2021-002.pdf). The APA page is an APA league glossary, not a scientific glossary or a worldwide rulebook.

## First-party demonstrations and empirical measurement

### Instructional / visual demonstrations

- [PoolDawg Slow Motion Billiards Series introduction](https://www.pooldawg.com/article/introducing-pooldawg-slow-motion-billiards-series/) says its camera filmed pool shots at up to 6,000 frames per second and describes footage on tip compression, chalk retention, shaft deflection, cue-ball spin, jumps, and breaks. Its [tip-compression video page](https://www.pooldawg.com/article/kamui-tip-compression/) compares soft, medium, hard, and phenolic tips. The series was produced by a billiards retailer with Florian “Venom” Kohler and Kuma Films. This is first-party footage and useful visualization; it is not a controlled report of impact forces, cue speed, or material performance, and tip hardness is not a soft/firm stroke scale.
- [The Snooker Gym’s “Ronnie’s Delivery | Start Slow…Finish Fast”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6O0SznRu0fg) is a coach-published, slow-motion professional delivery demonstration. It is useful to inspect cue rhythm and visible mechanics. The video description does not specify a calibrated frame rate or provide measured cue velocity; treat its “start slow/finish fast” framing as a coaching claim, not an empirical result.

### Original biomechanics studies (measurement evidence, not teaching standards)

- [Kornfeind et al. (2015), “Movement Variability of Professional Pool Billiards Players on Selected Tasks”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877705815014976) studied 20 elite pool players performing 18 defined follow, draw, stop, and break tasks. The paper reports 3D motion capture at 250 Hz, a 5,000 Hz high-speed camera, and a 5 kHz cue-butt accelerometer. This is direct evidence that cue motion can be measured with these methods and that player/task variability is material. It is an experimental setup, not a recommended home camera protocol or normative speed table.
- [Kong et al. (2021), “Acquiring Expertise in Precision Sport – What Can We Learn from an Elite Snooker Player?”](https://doi.org/10.5334/paah.111) used Vicon motion capture at 400 Hz with markers on the cue tip and butt, five snooker tasks, and three repetitions per task for one professional male player. Reported mean impact cue-tip speeds ranged from 1.24 m/s (warm-up) to 4.02 m/s (backspin). The paper labels its object-ball placements “short” and “long” but gives no universal distance thresholds. These are observed values from one player and a small, specific task set, not coaching bins or target speeds for all players.

### Table-speed measurement

- [Joe Waldron, “Pool Table Stimpmeter”](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/instructional-articles/pb-review/stimpmeter/) proposes (a) a lag-style timing check on a 9-foot table, with about seven seconds for a specified rail-to-rail return called “competition speed,” and (b) an inclined wooden ramp to release a ball and mark roll distances. The ramp uses a fixed geometry, clean table/ball, a stated room condition, repeated checks, and separate markings for a cloth-only roll and a two-diamond rail return. The author explicitly says no particular pool stimpmeter is required and the method is not widespread. Treat the values as a proposed local comparison method, not a governing-body standard. The article also notes humidity and loose rail bolts changed the measured return, so keep environmental and table state in the log.

## Practical measurement recommendation

For a research or game-physics record, keep continuous measurements and define each test before labeling it “soft,” “medium,” “long,” or “power.” Log cue-tip travel during the backswing, cue-tip speed just before impact, cue-ball launch speed/spin, cue-ball/object-ball placement, and the measured outcome. Use fixed camera views and the same cue, ball, table, and room setup across repeats. This adapts the PBIA video/sensor recommendations and the published motion-capture methods; it is a practical recommendation, not a PBIA/WPBSA protocol.

For table comparisons, name the observable: **cloth roll distance without a cushion**, **rail-return distance**, or **lag duration**. Repeat in both directions, keep the ball and surface clean, and record room/table conditions. The ramp method’s cloth-only and rail-inclusive readings answer different questions; do not collapse them into one unnamed “table speed.”

## Scope limits

- The WPBSA material is snooker-specific; the PBIA curriculum is for professional pool instruction; the APA glossary reflects APA league terminology. None is a universal authority on every cue-sport dialect.
- These source sets supply coach competencies, cueing recommendations, and examples of measurement. They do not establish a universal short/medium/long stroke taxonomy, quantitative soft/firm/power bands, or an official pocket-speed test.
- “First-party” here means the organization, coach, author, publisher, or footage producer is the source for its own standard or demonstration. The experimental papers are primary results; the instructional documents are standards or advice; commercial video is illustrative content.
