# Experimental-source scout: pool friction, throw, cloth, and cue

Research date: 2026-11-04. Scope: primary measured work outside the guide's existing core set. This is a source map for follow-up reading, not a new universal parameter set. “Measured” below means the authors report observations or apparatus results; model fits and recommendations remain separately labeled.

## Highest-value sources

| Question for the learning guide | Primary source and what it measured | Result worth carrying forward | Scope and limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How can rolling and sliding resistance be measured on an actual pool table, and which coefficients were identified?** | Thomas Nierhoff, Kerstin Heunisch, and Sandra Hirche, [“Strategic play for a pool-playing robot,” ARSO 2012, pp. 72–78](https://doi.org/10.1109/ARSO.2012.6213402), [TUM-hosted full paper](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1155344/988748.pdf). A ceiling camera tracks a single rolling ball; a separate weighted rack standing on three fixed pool balls is pulled along the cloth at controlled speed, with force measured by a JR3 force/torque sensor. | The paper reports a fitted rolling-resistance model that is nearly speed-independent above about 0.05 m/s and changes at very low speed. Its printed rolling fits switch at `0.0473 m/s`: `λ_ra = 0.0011` / `0.0075`, and `λ_rr = 0.1350` / `−0.0004`. The sliding fits are `λ_sa = 0.2014` / `0.2322`, and `λ_sr = 0.4763` / `0.0180`; their printed breakpoint is `0.672 m/s`. The equations define the different terms, so these values should be copied with their model definitions, not renamed as one generic friction coefficient. | This is a real-table pocket-pool calibration, but the accessible paper does not report the table dimensions; cloth type, age, humidity, temperature, and heating condition are also absent. Rolling data were noisy because the fitted force was small. The authors explicitly say ball–ball friction and restitution could not be identified with their setup and instead use literature values. There is also an internal breakpoint mismatch worth resolving from the PDF before reuse: the prose says both rolling and sliding behavior changes below `0.05 m/s`, while the printed sliding-fit equations switch at `0.672 m/s`. This is a strong calibration-method source and a poor basis for transferring its constants to another table. |
| **How do cue-ball speed and surface condition change cut-induced throw, including chalk-at-contact and the static-electricity claim?** | David Alciatore, [“Throw Follow-up: Part I: Cling,” *Billiards Digest*, July 2014](https://drdavepoolinfo.com/bd_articles/2014/july14.pdf). This is the experimenter's first-person report, with a frozen-ball setup: the cue ball hits the first ball squarely, which creates a repeatable center-to-edge half-ball hit on the second ball; the cue ball has no sidespin. | In the reported speed comparison, throw decreases from about `6°` at the slowest shot to about `3.5°` at the fastest. On the chalk-contact test, the clean/used balls produced `4.4°` and the chalk smudge at the contact point produced `6.5°` (about 50% more); the author notes these were different ball brands. Rubbing a ball on cloth to charge it, with paper attraction used as a charge check, changed the reported throw from `5.8°` to `5.6°`, which the author treats as insignificant and not an increase. The surface-treatment table reports `7.1°` for alcohol, `7.0°` for dish soap, `5.3°` for Aramith cleaner, `2.7°` for car wax, and `0.2°` for silicone spray; added chalk or light sanding did not exceed the roughly `7°` gearing limit in that setup. | Direct measured work on pool balls and a useful answer to “what can a high-friction patch do?” It is a practitioner-magazine report, not a peer-reviewed paper. The note does not state shot counts, uncertainty, randomization, controlled ball speed for each treatment, or a treatment washout protocol. Treat the figures as demonstrations with condition-specific magnitudes, not population estimates or advice to apply products to playing balls. The different-brand comparison on the chalk test is an explicit confound. |
| **Where does experimental throw peak as the contact geometry changes?** | Michihisa Sudo, Nao Ninomiya, and Yoshio Terashima, [“Collision Analysis of Two Billiard Balls by High-Speed Camera,” *Journal of the Visualization Society of Japan* 23, Supplement 1 (2003), 135–138](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jvs1990/23/Supplement1/23_Supplement1_135/_article/-char/en). J-STAGE identifies the article as free access and links its PDF. | The journal abstract says they used high-speed video plus digital image processing to quantify trajectories and found maximum throw at a `30°` contact angle. This is a distinct experimental result from treating ideal equal-mass collision directions as a fixed right angle. | The four-page article is Japanese; the public English abstract supplies the exact result, but I could not verify the full methods, ball type, speed range, repeat count, uncertainty, or their definition of “contact angle” from the English record. Use it as a lead and cite the 30° result with that boundary. |
| **Do chalk and cloth cleaning conditions alter motion phases before and after a ball collision?** | S. Descartes, J. Lachambre, and C. Desrayaud, [“Experimental Evidence of Friction Effect Involved in Playing French Billiard,” Leeds–Lyon Symposium on Tribology (2019), proceedings PDF](https://leeds-lyon2019.sciencesconf.org/255609/billard_2019V2.pdf). They used a Phantom Miro eX4 high-speed camera at `1900 fps`, tracked cue-ball motion, and examined cue tip, balls, chalk, and cloth with optical and scanning electron microscopy. | For follow and draw shots, they report a sequence of initial sliding, mixed rolling/sliding, pure rolling after the first ball collision, then renewed mixed rolling/sliding. The phases depended on shot effect, chalk presence, and cloth cleaning condition. | This is tribology evidence on a French carom setup, not American pool: `61.5 mm` phenolic-resin balls, three-ball table without pockets, single-layer `3 mm` leather tip, and a `1.5 m` wooden cue. The short proceedings paper reports qualitative dependencies, not a reusable coefficient, full raw data, uncertainty, or enough trial detail to estimate effect size. |
| **Do cue stiffness and tip hardness measurably affect ball launch and spin?** | Qiuyun Yin, Lingling Hu, Long Chen, and T. X. Yu, [“Objective evaluation on performance of billiard cue in hitting ball,” *International Journal of Impact Engineering* 158 (2021), 104020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijimpeng.2021.104020). The publisher describes an experimental cue-launch device and testing at central and follow-shot offsets. | The paper's abstract reports that moderate shaft stiffness had better force-transfer performance than softer or harder tested shafts, and the softer tested tip produced greater rotational velocity than the harder tip in the follow-shot comparison. The device compares ball speed for a controlled cue speed, a helpful operational definition for “cue efficiency.” | It is an experiment on the tested cues and apparatus, not a general ranking of cue products. The public publisher record does not expose the full methods/results tables here. The abstract says rotational velocity was derived from measured translational speed, so that result is not an independent direct spin measurement. The authors specify snooker cues in the experimental description; don't silently treat the sample as American pool cues. |

## Useful but weak calibration lead

John Schmidt, [“An Experimental Determination of the Kinetic Friction Coefficient of a Miniature Pool Table” (College of Wooster, 2022)](https://physics.wooster.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/JuniorIS_2022_Schmidt.pdf), reports 20 trials on a `1.010 × 0.490 m` miniature table and a weighted fit of `0.288 ± 0.002`. The author also reports a trial standard deviation of `0.074` (25.6% of the mean), a motion-sensor time resolution of `0.05 s`, unknown transfer to a full table, and acknowledges possible violations of its assumptions. More importantly for terminology, the experiment tracks a rolling ball and derives `a/g` using a Coulomb static-friction force equation; that is not a direct measurement of the kinetic sliding coefficient. Its result should not be compared numerically with sliding-friction values or used as a full-size-table constant. It can be read as an educational apparatus attempt and a warning to inspect what the symbol `μ` physically represents.

## Evidence gaps and a better source trail

- I found no open primary experimental series that independently maps **cloth nap direction, humidity, wear, and cleaning condition** to ball deceleration or spin decay on a full-size WPA-style pocket-pool table. The TUM work measures one real pool table without reporting those conditions; the French-billiard tribology paper varies chalk/cleaning qualitatively but uses larger carom balls; the magazine throw tests vary ball-surface treatment rather than table humidity or nap.
- A manufacturer-hosted summary of a reported FIBiS cloth test describes a ramp/roll index, slate temperature, ambient humidity, and a 5% decrease after one week with the heater off, but the public page provides no raw humidity values, repeated measurements, uncertainty, or the underlying Federation report. Keep this as a retrieval lead, not a calibrated finding: [Royal Pro's FIBiS test summary](https://www.royalpro.gr/official-test-of-royal-pro-billiard-cloth-from-fibis-italian-billiard-federation/).
- Best next source request: obtain the full FIBiS laboratory/report sheet or a cloth-maker's instrumented test protocol, then run a controlled full-size pool-table series that logs cloth type/age/nap direction, ambient RH and temperature, slate temperature/heater state, cleaning, ball material/diameter/mass, and repeated launch speeds. Track both center translation and ball orientation so rolling drag, sliding friction, and spin decay remain separate observables. This would close a real gap rather than append another uncalibrated “table speed” constant.

## Fit to the existing guide

The strongest additions are the TUM full-size table measurement setup and its explicit statement that ball–ball friction/restitution were unidentifiable, plus the Alciatore treatment table as a bounded primary demonstration of condition-dependent throw. Sudo et al. supply a narrow high-speed-camera throw result; Descartes et al. supply a different carom/tribology view of chalk and cloth condition; Yin et al. add a controlled cue-device experiment. None supports replacing the guide's existing source-specific evidence with universal coefficients.
